Finding devices and free IPs on an unfamiliar network

17 April 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026

You turn up to a venue, plug into their network, and need a static IP. Or you're handed a system nobody has documented, and half the devices are unresponsive. Or a console won't see a media server and you need to know whether it's on the network at all. Same question each time: what's on this LAN, and which addresses are free?

Four ways to answer that on a Mac.

1. arp - what has recently talked to you

In Terminal:

arp -a -n

This lists every device your Mac has recently exchanged traffic with, by IP and MAC address. It's fast and cheap but only shows devices that have actually been talking. A device that's online but quiet won't appear until you poke it. Useful for a quick "what's around", not a complete picture.

2. Ping sweep - crude and effective

To see which addresses respond on the typical 192.168.1.x subnet:

( for i in {1..254}; do (ping -c 1 -W 200 192.168.1.$i >/dev/null && echo "192.168.1.$i up") & done; wait ) | sort -t . -k 4 -n

This pings all 254 addresses at once, waits for them, and prints every address that replied, in order. It takes a few seconds. One ping after another would take minutes on a mostly empty subnet, because macOS waits about a second on each address that doesn't answer. Then run arp -a -n again and you'll have a more complete list. The catch: anything configured to ignore ICMP pings (some IP cameras, a few consoles, anything behind a strict firewall) won't show up. It's a baseline, not a guarantee.

3. Bonjour browser - for anything that announces itself

Most modern AV gear (printers, AirPlay devices, QLab instances, Dante devices in Dante Controller) broadcasts its presence via Bonjour. You can see this without installing anything:

dns-sd -B _services._dns-sd._udp

or browse a specific service type:

dns-sd -B _qlab._tcp

This finds devices by name rather than IP, which is often more useful in an AV context. "Lighting console" tells you more than "192.168.1.47".

Picking a free IP

Once you know what's on the network, picking an address is quick. A few rules:

  1. Stay inside the subnet you're already on. If the router is 192.168.1.1 and responding devices sit in 192.168.1.0/24, don't invent an address in 192.168.2.x.
  2. Avoid the low addresses (1-10) and the high ones (250-254). Those are where routers, DHCP servers, and management gear tend to live. Sit in the middle.
  3. Double-check with ping before you commit. If your chosen address replies before you've configured yourself, someone else has it.
  4. If the network has DHCP, ask the gateway for its lease range. Sit outside it, not inside it. A static address inside a DHCP pool will eventually collide.
