Target curves in Smaart: what they are and how to use one

5 July 2026

Point a measurement mic at a system, pull up a transfer function in Smaart, and the obvious instinct is to EQ until the trace is flat. Do that to a full-range PA at show level and it will sound thin, harsh, and short on low end. Flat on the analyser is not flat to the ear - which is why system engineers tune to a target curve instead.

What a target curve is

A target curve is the frequency response shape you actually want the system to measure at the mic position - a reference trace you overlay on your measurement and EQ towards. Instead of chasing a flat line, you're chasing a deliberate shape that accounts for how people hear music at performance levels in a real room.

Once it is loaded, Smaart draws it alongside your live measurement, which is why people also call it the target trace. It is the same thing: a stored curve sitting on the transfer function as a reference, not a filter or anything that touches the audio.

Why flat sounds wrong

A few reasons flat measurements do not equal flat perception:

  • Loudness perception. At high SPL our hearing is relatively more sensitive to mid frequencies. A system that measures flat at 100 dB reads as bright and bass-light compared to the same material at home listening level.
  • Room and distance. Recordings are mixed on monitors in controlled rooms at moderate level. A PA in a reverberant room at distance behaves nothing like that reference, and high-frequency energy in the room builds differently to low.
  • Expectation. Decades of live sound, cinema (the X-curve), and studio practice have converged on the same finding: audiences expect a gentle downward tilt with some low-end support.

Common starting shapes

There is no single correct target - it depends on the system, the room, and the genre - but most working curves are variations on the same idea:

  • A downward tilt through the mids and highs, typically 0.5 to 1 dB per octave.
  • A low-frequency shelf, often somewhere between +3 and +6 dB below roughly 100-200 Hz, more for EDM and pop, less for speech and corporate.
  • A gentle high-frequency roll-off above 8-10 kHz - partly taste, partly a nod to air absorption over distance.

Speech-only systems usually want less of everything: a flatter mid band and restrained low end for intelligibility.

Using one in Smaart

Smaart lets you load a target curve and display it against your live transfer function trace, so tuning becomes "EQ towards the overlay" rather than remembering a shape in your head. The practical workflow:

  • Start from a known shape rather than a blank slate - a house curve you like, or a published starting point.
  • Get the system's overall trend to follow the target with broad strokes: crossover alignment and wide EQ first.
  • Resist matching every wiggle. The target is a trend line; narrow dips are usually room interaction that EQ can't fix.
  • Verify with program material you know, then adjust the curve - not just the EQ - if the shape itself is wrong for the room.

Building and editing the curve itself

The awkward part has always been authoring the curve. Text files of frequency and magnitude pairs are easy to export but miserable to design by hand, and a curve you sketched for one rig usually needs reshaping for the next room.

That's the gap Target Trace fills: draw and edit target curves visually on a Mac - drag control points, draw freehand, simplify a trace you've imported - then export directly in Smaart and REW formats. Keep a folder of house curves per system or per genre and adapt them per room instead of starting over.
