Target curves in Smaart: what they are and how to use one

5 July 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026

A Smaart target curve is a reference line you load over your measurement and EQ toward. It is rarely flat, because a PA tuned to a flat trace sounds thin and harsh at show level. The instinct with a transfer function on screen is to EQ until the trace is flat. Flat on the analyser is not flat to the ear.

What a target curve is

A target curve is the frequency response shape you want the system to measure at the mic position - a reference line you overlay on your measurement and EQ towards. Instead of chasing a flat line, you chase a deliberate shape that allows for the room, the distance and what the audience expects to hear.

Smaart keeps two kinds of reference: a target curve is a .crv file managed in its Target Curves window, while a target trace is a captured measurement (.srf for spectrum, .trf for transfer function) loaded into the Data Bar like any other trace. Either one sits on the plot as a reference only. Neither is a filter, and neither touches the audio.

Why flat sounds wrong

  • Air loss. High frequencies lose energy to the air over distance, and the loss changes with temperature and humidity. Boost the top end until it reads flat at the mix position and the seats nearer the stage get it harsh.
  • Directivity and reflections. Boxes and arrays are far more directional at high frequencies than low. The low end spills into the walls, ceiling and back of the room and returns as reflected energy, while the top end mostly goes where the boxes point. A mic in the room hears that as a downward tilt. EQ it flat and you are cutting the bottom and boosting the top against what the system does on axis.
  • Expectation. Audiences at a music show expect a gentle downward tilt with real low-end weight. Cinema settled on a tilted target too: the X-curve.

Common starting shapes

There is no correct target. Most working curves start from the same few moves:

  • A downward tilt through the mids and highs, typically 0.5 to 1 dB per octave.
  • A low-frequency shelf, often somewhere between +3 and +6 dB below roughly 100-200 Hz, more for EDM and pop, less for speech and corporate.
  • A haystack - flat through the mids, then a ramp that climbs from a few hundred hertz down to the bottom of the range. It goes by the size of the lift: a 9 dB haystack for a large music system, 15 dB for a high-impact one.
  • A gentle high-frequency roll-off above 8-10 kHz - partly taste, partly a nod to air absorption over distance.

Speech-only systems usually want less of everything: a flatter mid band and restrained low end for intelligibility. For more on picking a shape for a live PA, see house curves for live sound.

Loading a target curve into Smaart

Open the Target Curves window from Options > Target Curves (Alt/Opt+X), pick the Spectrum or Transfer Function tab to match the curve, and press Import. The X key shows and hides target curves on the plot. Transfer function target curves need a measurement-capable edition of Smaart 9.4 or later; before that, a captured TF trace was the only way to overlay a TF reference. From Smaart 9.5 you can also right-click a trace in the Data Bar and choose Export as Target Curve.

Smaart reads its TargetCurves folder when it starts. A .crv saved into that folder while Smaart is running does not show up until you restart Smaart or bring it in with Import.

Tuning to the curve

With the curve on the plot, tuning becomes "EQ towards the overlay" rather than remembering a shape in your head.

  • Start from a known shape rather than a blank slate - a house curve you like, or a published starting point.
  • Get the system's overall trend to follow the target with broad strokes: crossover alignment and wide EQ first.
  • Resist matching every wiggle. The target is a trend line; narrow dips are usually room interaction that EQ can't fix.
  • Verify with program material you know, then adjust the curve - not just the EQ - if the shape itself is wrong for the room.

Building and editing the curve itself

Authoring the curve is the slow part. A .crv is two tab-separated columns of frequency and level under a short header, and Rational Acoustics warns that spaces or commas in place of tabs can crash Smaart on import. Text files like that are easy to export but miserable to design by hand, and a curve you sketched for one system usually needs reshaping for the next room.

Target Trace is a Mac app for that job, and it can start from the sound you already have. Love the sound of the PA right now? Start Live Trace in the editor and grab the exact moment you want. With Smaart's API switched on, Hold freezes the trace and Grab turns it into your curve, point for point. Tidy it with Point Spacing (1/1 to 1/24 octave) and save it as a Smaart .crv. Save it for later, edit your live grabs at any time. A REW .txt is an export only, so keep the .crv as the copy you edit.

Working on one screen? Float the live trace over your EQ page and Lock it to that app. See your EQ changes in real time, right on your EQ. Locked, the trace moves with the app window and clicks pass straight through to the EQ underneath. Set Scale to 24 for LA Network Manager or 20 for QL Editor, and Target Trace remembers it per app.

Starting cold instead? Pick a preset (Flat, Tilt, 9 dB or 15 dB Haystack, X-Curve), drag control points or draw freehand, Flatten a rough section, and tick library curves to overlay them, TF curves with their saved tolerance bands. Keep a folder of house curves per system or per genre and adapt them per room instead of starting over.
