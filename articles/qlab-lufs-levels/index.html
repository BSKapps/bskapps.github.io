Setting consistent QLab levels with LUFS

5 July 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026

Measure each file's integrated LUFS, subtract it from your target, and set the difference as the cue's level in QLab. Do that for every cue and they all play back at about the same loudness, whatever state the files arrived in.

Every corporate show and awards night has the same audio problem: the walk-in playlist came from one place, the stings came from another, the client supplied their own sizzle-reel audio, and someone exported the walk-up music from a DAW at a completely different level. Drop them all into QLab, hit Go, and the first cue whispers while the second one takes the roof off.

The usual fix is to sit there in tech scrubbing through every cue, nudging faders by ear. It works, sort of, until a cue you didn't check fires at show level.

Why peak level doesn't help

Most files you're handed are already peak-normalised - the loudest sample sits at or near 0 dBFS. That tells you almost nothing about how loud the file sounds. A heavily compressed dance track and a dynamic orchestral piece can both peak at 0 dBFS and differ by 15 dB or more in perceived loudness. Matching peaks matches nothing.

What LUFS measures

LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale) is a loudness measure weighted to how we hear, defined in ITU BS.1770 and used by the EBU R128 broadcast standard. Instead of the loudest sample, it measures average loudness over the whole file. Two files at the same integrated LUFS sound about the same volume, regardless of genre, compression, or where they came from.

Common targets:

  • -14 LUFS - roughly where streaming services normalise music playback.
  • -18 LUFS - Lab Assistant's default for theatre and live playback, leaving headroom on the desk.
  • -23 LUFS - European broadcast delivery (EBU R128).
  • -24 LUFS - US broadcast delivery (ATSC A/85).

The exact number matters less than everything being at the same number. Once every cue matches, one fader on the console rides the whole show.

Levelling cues to a target by hand

  • Measure the integrated LUFS of each file with a loudness meter or analysis tool. Most DAWs have one.
  • Subtract the measurement from your target. A file measuring -22 LUFS with a -18 target needs +4 dB.
  • Type that value into the cue's main level in QLab.

Watch QLab's level cap. A cue cannot go above the workspace's maximum volume limit, +12 dB by default, so a very quiet file can need more lift than QLab allows. Take the same amount, say 10 dB, off every cue so the quiet ones still fit, and make it up at the desk.

Setting the cue level rather than re-rendering the file keeps the original audio untouched, so there's no generation loss and no re-export if the target changes.

This works fine for five cues. For an awards night with sixty walk-ups it's an hour of measuring and typing, and it all has to happen again when the running order changes on the morning of the show.

Things that still need ears

A LUFS pass gets every cue to the same starting point. It doesn't replace judgement:

  • Stings and impacts are often meant to be louder than beds. Level everything to target first, then push the stings up a consistent few dB.
  • Speech-under-music beds usually want to sit lower than the music target.
  • Very short files (under a second or so) measure less reliably - give hits and buttons a quick ear check.

Treat it as the starting level, then use your ears.
