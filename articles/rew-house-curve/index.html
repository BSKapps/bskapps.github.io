House curves in REW: why flat sounds thin and what to use instead

5 July 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026

A house curve in REW is a text file of frequency and dB offsets that REW adds to its EQ target, so the filters steer the room toward a shape instead of flat. Without one, REW aims at its target type, flat for a full-range speaker, unless you tick Add room curve. A room corrected to flat often measures clean and sounds thin, dry and short of the low end you bought the subwoofer for.

What a house curve is

A house curve is the in-room frequency response you actually want at the listening position. When you give REW a house curve, its EQ engine generates filters that steer the measured response towards that shape instead of a flat line.

Why flat in-room disappoints

Speakers are designed to measure flat anechoically - on-axis, no room. Put a good flat speaker in a normal room and the steady-state response at the listening position naturally tilts downward: the room reinforces low frequencies and absorbs high ones, and you sit in the combined direct-plus-reflected field. That gentle downward slope is what a well-regarded system measures in-room. Force the in-room response flat with EQ and you've effectively boosted the treble and cut the bass relative to what the speaker was voiced to do.

Common starting shapes

  • The Harman curve - from Harman listening tests (Olive, Welti and McMullin, AES 2013). Listeners adjusted a flat in-room response and on average preferred about +6 dB of bass below roughly 100 Hz, with slightly less treble. It is a home listening result, from a listening room, not a venue.
  • A simple tilt - a straight slope of around -0.5 to -1 dB per octave across the band. Less low-end emphasis than Harman; a common choice for nearfield and studio-adjacent setups.
  • Flat-with-bass-shelf - leave the mids and highs alone and add only the low shelf. Popular for home theatre where the goal is impact without changing tonal character.

All of these are starting points. Room size, speaker directivity, listening distance, and taste all move the answer. For a live PA, see house curves for live sound.

Loading one into REW

REW reads a house curve as a plain text file: a frequency in Hz and an offset in dB on each line, with frequencies in ascending order. Since REW 5.30 you choose the file in the Target settings of the EQ window; older versions set it in Preferences. REW adds the house curve on top of the target type you pick there, and the filters it generates aim at the combined shape.

An example file, for illustration only - +6 dB below 100 Hz easing to 0 dB at 300 Hz, then a 3 dB fall to 20 kHz:

20 6.0
100 6.0
300 0.0
20000 -3.0
  • REW interpolates between the points you give it, so the file needs enough points to describe the shape smoothly - a handful of points makes angular corners, not a curve. Tick Use logarithmic interpolation and the lines between points run straight on REW's log frequency axis.
  • Correct the big trends and the low-frequency room modes; don't chase narrow high-frequency dips, which are usually interference effects that EQ makes worse.
  • Re-listen and iterate on the curve, not just the filters. If everything sounds dull, the tilt is too steep; if it's fatiguing, not steep enough.
