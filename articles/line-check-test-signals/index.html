Line check test signals: what to send and what it tells you

18 August 2026

A line check answers three questions: is the signal arriving, is it arriving on the right channel, and is it arriving the right way up. Music answers none of them reliably. A test signal answers one each, quickly, and from the middle of the room rather than hunched over a laptop.

Pink noise, and why it is the default

Pink noise carries equal energy per octave, which is a way of saying it sounds evenly weighted across the spectrum rather than hissy like white noise. White is flat per hertz, so it piles energy into the top octaves - useful as an FFT reference, unpleasant as a thing to stand in front of. Red or brown noise slopes further down at 6 dB per octave and is what you want when the question is about subs.

For a line check the point is not analysis, it is coverage: pink through a box tells you the driver is passing full range, and a band-limited version around a chosen centre frequency isolates one part of the system. Band-limited pink into a sub is the classic version of that trick.

Identifying a stereo pair by ear

Two signals answer the "which one is left" question without touching a meter, and both run at 1 kHz on both channels at -18 dBFS for the EBU reference:

  • The EBU stereo ident - the left channel drops out for 250 ms every three seconds while the right runs continuously. The channel with the gap is left. It doubles as a phase check when summed to mono: a correct pair dips about 6 dB through the gap, while a polarity-reversed pair cancels to silence and the gap turns into a short pip.
  • GLITS - the same idea with a distinct pattern per channel, so you can identify either side on its own rather than by comparison.

Both beat shouting "left" into a talkback mic, and both survive being heard from the back of a room.

Polarity, and reading a pop

A polarity pop is a positive-going pulse. The first movement of the cone should be outward, and a polarity tester or a trained ear at the box will tell you if it is not. Pulse width matters more than people expect: a very short pulse of around half a millisecond suits tweeters and HF sections, a standard few-millisecond pulse covers full-range boxes, and a much longer one is needed before a subwoofer will reproduce it at all. Sending an HF-length pop at a sub and concluding the sub is broken is a common and avoidable mistake.

Alignment by ear

A sharp broadband click sent to two outputs at once - mains and delays, or delays and fills - is a fast sanity check on time alignment. Standing where the two zones meet, in sync it reads as one click; out of sync it smears or doubles. It will not replace a transfer function measurement, but it catches a delay entered in feet when the system is set to metres before anyone has plugged in a measurement mic.

When it becomes measurement

Once an analyser is involved the requirements change. Transfer function work wants a signal that is periodic at the analyser's FFT frame - 32K, 64K or 128K - and looped, so the average settles to high coherence without spectral leakage smearing the result. Periodic pink is the general-purpose choice; a cycle-synchronised log sweep from 20 Hz to 20 kHz suits impulse response work. A file exported for this needs to be exactly one frame long at the capture sample rate, with no end fades, or the loop point itself shows up in the measurement.
