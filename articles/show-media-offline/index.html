Walk-in music and playback video: getting show media offline

18 August 2026

Every tech who has run walk-in music off a streaming app has the same story: the venue wifi drops, or the free tier fires an ad into the PA, or the phone rings and the track ducks in front of two hundred people. Streaming is fine for listening. It is not a playback system, and the fix is boring and total - everything the show needs lives on the show machine before doors.

What actually needs to be local

More than people plan for. A typical corporate or theatre day wants:

  • Walk-in and walk-out music - usually an hour or more each, because nothing exposes a short playlist like a delayed start.
  • Stings, bumpers and award beds - short, cued, often needed at a specific length.
  • Video content - showreels, sponsor loops, VT packages, sometimes handed over as a link rather than a file.
  • Reference tracks - the material you tune and check the system with, which should be the same tracks every time so you know what they are meant to sound like.

That last one matters more than it gets credit for. Tuning a room against whatever happens to be streaming is guesswork. Tuning against tracks you have heard on fifty systems tells you what the room is doing.

Formats that behave

Once the media is local, the format decides how much trouble it causes:

  • Audio for QLab - uncompressed WAV or AIFF. Compressed files decode on the fly, which is usually fine and occasionally is not, and the failure lands mid-show rather than in prep.
  • Sample rate - match the workspace and the interface. A 44.1 kHz file in a 48 kHz session gets converted somewhere, and where that happens is not always your choice.
  • Video - a mezzanine codec like ProRes for anything being scrubbed, cut or played back reliably. Long-GOP web formats are built for streaming, not for seeking to a cue point.
  • Loudness - a playlist assembled from mixed sources arrives at wildly different levels. Level it once during prep rather than riding a fader all night.

Metadata is not optional

Forty files called track01 in a cue list is its own kind of show risk. Artist and title tags survive into QLab cue names, into REAPER item names, and into the paperwork you hand the next operator. Artwork matters less on the audio side, but it is the difference between a browsable library and a folder you dread opening.

A workflow that holds up

  • Build the playlists well before the day, when a missing track is an inconvenience rather than a crisis.
  • Pull everything down in one pass, in the format you actually need, rather than converting later under time pressure.
  • Level the set as a set, so walk-in does not lurch between songs.
  • Keep a standing reference-track folder that never changes, separate from show content.
  • Copy the whole lot to the backup machine at the same time. Media that only exists on one Mac is not prepared, it is pending.

A note on rights

Prepping media offline does not change what you are allowed to play. Public performance of commercial music at an event is covered by the venue's or the client's licensing, and production libraries have their own terms. That is a paperwork question, not a technical one, but it is worth asking before the day rather than after.
