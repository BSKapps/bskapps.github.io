Audio file formats for QLab: what to use and what to avoid

5 July 2026

QLab will play almost anything you drop on it - WAV, AIFF, CAF, MP3, AAC, and whatever else macOS knows how to decode. That flexibility is great in the rehearsal room and a trap on show day, because "it plays" and "it plays predictably every single time" are different standards. The files a client emails you were made for convenience, not playback.

The safe choice: uncompressed, at the show's sample rate

For anything that matters, use WAV or AIFF:

  • No decoding surprises. Uncompressed PCM has nothing to unpack - seeking is sample-accurate, durations are exact, and the CPU cost is trivial.
  • 48 kHz is the usual show standard, matching video and most playback rigs. Whatever your rig runs at, make every file match it - resampling on the fly is work the machine doesn't need to do during a show.
  • 16-bit or 24-bit both fine. 24-bit if the source has it, but nobody has ever lost a show to 16-bit playback.

Disk space stopped being an argument years ago. A three-hour show's worth of stereo WAVs is a few gigabytes on a machine with hundreds free.

MP3 and AAC: fine until they aren't

Compressed files mostly work, and for a walk-in playlist nobody will hear the difference. The trouble is behavioural, not sonic:

  • VBR MP3s are the classic trap. Variable bitrate files have no fixed relationship between position in the file and position in time, so reported durations can be wrong and seeks land off-target - a slice or a start-time offset in QLab may not hit where you set it. If a cue's waveform doesn't match what you hear, a VBR MP3 is the first suspect. If MP3 is unavoidable, use constant bitrate (CBR).
  • Encoder delay padding. MP3 and AAC add a short stretch of silence at the front of the file as a by-product of encoding, which matters the moment cues need tight timing against each other.

The habit that avoids all of it: treat compressed files as source material, and convert them to WAV before they go anywhere near the workspace.

Mono, stereo, and channel counts

Know what each file actually is before it hits your output patch. A mono file in a workspace patched for stereo pairs behaves differently to a stereo file, and a stereo file with identical channels is mono in disguise taking up two channels of routing.
