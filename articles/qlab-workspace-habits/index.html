QLab workspace habits from real shows

12 April 2026

A QLab workspace at the start of tech is a clean thing. Nicely numbered, sensibly named, everything fading how the designer intended. By day five it's three hundred cues deep with inconsistent numbering, a couple of orphaned groups, and at least one cue nobody remembers adding. The habits below are the small disciplines that keep that drift under control, and a few traps that have caught me out personally.

Assume the levels are never the same

Across a season you'll get files from the composer, the sound designer, the foley editor, maybe the director's phone voice-memo. They're never mixed to the same reference. One cue peaks at -1 dBFS, the next sits at -18 dBFS with no explanation. If you trust the filenames and don't listen, the show will be loud in places it shouldn't be.

Pass every new file through QLab at a known starting point. Same output patch, same master level, same room. Set a consistent cue level based on what you hear, not what the waveform looks like. Do it once per asset, not cue by cue.

Default new audio cues to -10 dB

In QLab, set the template for new audio cues to start at -10 dB instead of 0 dB. One caveat upfront: it flips which failure mode bites you. New files dragged in at -10 won't blast the room before you check them, but if you ever run a level pass over the workspace - by hand or with Lab Assistant with a negative offset - anything still sitting at 0 dB ends up punching out well above the rest. The fix is to keep every cue starting at the same reference, so the level pass behaves predictably.

The upside is headroom. Quiet tracks have somewhere to go: a quiet voiceover that needed +6 dB to sit right was previously stuck against the 0 dB ceiling, with no clean way to push it. Starting at -10 means you can lift the quiet ones and lower the hot ones without anything pinned to a wall.

The dB-offset approach is what Figure 53 themselves recommend for QLab levelling, but in QLab it lives inside a built-in script you have to dig out of the workspace. Lab Assistant exposes the same offset and a full range of LUFS targets as actual controls, so you set the values once and apply them across cues without going scripting. Pick the target and offset that match how you mix and stick with them for the show. There's a longer write-up on why loudness beats peak level for playback: Setting consistent QLab levels with LUFS.

Mono files will catch you out

This one has caught me out more than once. Most apps play a mono file equally out the left and right outputs - you'd never think about it. QLab doesn't. A mono file gets sent to output 1 only by default. Output 1 might be stage left, stage centre, or an amplifier that isn't even on tonight. You don't catch it from the op position because the audio is playing - you can see the meters, you can hear something near you. It's only when you walk the house that you realise it's coming from one speaker, or from the wrong side, or from an amp that isn't even on tonight.

Two habits that fix it:

  • Check every new audio cue's patch routing. If it's mono, either explicitly route it to the outputs you want or convert it to interleaved stereo. Don't trust defaults.
  • Do a dry run of every audio cue from the house, on the actual system, before tech starts. Not from the op's seat with headphones.

This is exactly what the mono-routing module in Lab Assistant exists for - it walks the workspace and forces every mono cue to play out both outputs, so you don't have to remember to check. Set the Go button minimum time

Workspace Settings, General. There's a minimum time between Go presses, and it's worth setting deliberately rather than leaving it. Something like 500 ms protects you against the moment a stage manager's finger slips on the clicker, or your own Spacebar bounces. It won't save you from everything, but it will save you from the class of mistake where you fire cue 50 twice in a second and the show goes red.

Set a panic fade time before you need it

Workspace Settings, Workspace, has a panic fade duration. The default is short enough to feel like a hard cut. Set it to 3 or 4 seconds. The day you reach for Escape because something has gone catastrophically wrong, you do not want the entire bed of sound to cut instantly and leave the room in silence with everyone staring at the booth. Three to four seconds of fade out lets the house recover gracefully instead of slamming to silence.

And if the panic itself is the emergency - a runaway cue, feedback, a wrong file blasting out - double-hit Escape. QLab treats the second press as a hard stop: everything cuts immediately, panic fade ignored. Muscle memory worth practising at home.

Walk every continue mode by hand

Every cue has a continue mode: Do Not Continue, Auto-Continue, Auto-Follow. These determine whether the next cue waits for another Go or fires automatically. Get them wrong and the show stops cold or fires twice.

Walk the cue list once, end to end, in Show mode. Every cue's continue mode should be a deliberate choice, not the default from when you first dragged the file in. If the designer intended two sound effects to play together, both should fire from one Go with an Auto-Continue. If a music cue should wait for the SM's call, it should explicitly say so.

Trim cues manually before tech, not during

A five-second intro on a two-minute cue is five seconds of dead time the SM has to call early. Trim it. A piece of room tone that runs for 30 seconds after the line is dead tail that fades on the next cue's entry and muddies the transition. Trim it. QLab's in-cue start and end time fields exist for exactly this - you don't have to touch the file.

Do this before tech starts, with the designer present if possible. It tightens a show faster than any other single thing you can do without touching the content.

Number on tens, name for what they do

Number cues in tens from day one. 10, 20, 30. When the director asks for something between 10 and 20, it's 15. Between 15 and 20 it's 17. Three digits is a stop sign: rebuild the numbering rather than invent 12.5.

Name cues for what they do. Thunder - top of act 2 beats SFX 12 every time, and costs you nothing at read-through.

The clean-up problem

Renumbering forty cues, applying consistent fade and stop actions, batching level changes, all by hand, is the job no one wants. It's also what turns a messy workspace into a reliable one.

Save a clean copy before every run

Before the first tech run each day, save a dated copy of the workspace. Show v03 - 2026-04-12.qlab5. If you or the designer wreck something during the run, you have a known-good state ten minutes old. QLab's undo is fine for the last few moves. It's not a recovery strategy for a bad half hour.

None of this is clever. It's friction removed and a couple of traps sign-posted. Time you don't spend hunting for a cue or explaining a mono-file mistake during tech is time on the actual show.
