House curves for live sound: what shape to tune a PA to

3 October 2026

A house curve is the frequency response you EQ a PA toward instead of flat. On most live systems it rises in the low end and falls away at the top. There is no correct one.

Tune first, then tone

Bob McCarthy splits the job in two. Tuning is the objective part: levels, delays, crossovers and zone EQ, so every seat hears the same system. Toning is the global EQ that sets how it sounds, and he treats that as taste rather than something you can get wrong.

A house curve belongs to the toning half. Put it on top of a system whose zones do not match and you move the problem around without fixing it. Get the zones matching first.

Why a PA does not end up flat

  • Air takes the top. High frequencies lose level over distance, the loss grows with frequency, and it changes with temperature and humidity. L-Acoustics puts it at nearly 50 dB at 20 kHz over 100 m at 20 C and 40% humidity. Line array processors carry air compensation filters for the long-throw boxes - use those for the far seats rather than boosting the whole system.
  • The low end builds. Subs and low-frequency boxes couple when they sit close together, and most rooms ring on longer at low frequencies than at high ones.
  • Taste and genre. For music, most engineers want the low end sitting ahead of the mids. How far ahead depends on the show: more for dance and pop, less for speech.
  • Loud shows need less lift than you think. The ear's response flattens as level rises, so low end that sounds right at a quiet line check can be too much at show level. Judge the curve at show level.

Common starting shapes

These are starting points, not answers. Each one is a preset in Target Trace, so the numbers are the ones it draws.

  • Haystack. A low-end lift that rises below about 250 Hz and stays flat above it. Target Trace has two: +9 dB at 30 Hz for a typical large PA, and +15 dB at 30 Hz for high-impact systems.
  • Tilt. A straight line falling from low to high through a pivot frequency, with the total amount set by you. Add a sub bump when you want extra weight below the tilt.
  • Flat. A 0 dB reference, with an optional sub bump.
  • X-curve. The cinema curve from SMPTE ST 202: flat to 2 kHz, then about 3 dB per octave down, steeper above 10 kHz. It is a cinema standard, not a live sound shape - use it for screens.

Pick one, tune to it for a few shows, and move points where your ears disagree. Keep the versions that work for each system and genre.
