Virtual soundcheck in REAPER

7 July 2026

A virtual soundcheck is simple to describe: record every input channel during a soundcheck or a show, then play those recordings back into the console down the same channels, and mix as if the band were on stage. The band can be at dinner. You can solo the snare for ten minutes without anyone glaring at you. It is the single best use of a multitrack rig that most engineers never get around to.

What the console needs

Any desk with a multichannel card or USB interface can do this. Every current platform (dLive, Avantis, SQ, CL, QL, Rivage, Wing, and the rest) has a per-channel or global input switch between the preamp and the card return. Record from the sends, flip the switch, and channel 12 now hears the recording of channel 12 instead of the microphone. Check where your desk taps the send: most send the signal post-preamp, so the recording bakes the preamp gain in. That is fine, just resist re-gaining on playback, and do your fine trim digitally.

What REAPER needs

Two things, and they are the same two things a live recording needs, which is the point.

  • Inputs patched 1-to-1. One mono track per console channel, record armed, input N on track N.
  • Outputs patched 1-to-1. Each track also sends to hardware output N, with the master send off so nothing sums. Track 12 plays back down channel 12 and nowhere else.

Patch both directions when you build the session, either from a pre-patched track template or with a script that does the walk for you, and virtual soundcheck stops being a setup job. Record during soundcheck, press play, flip the console to card returns, and you are mixing.

A workflow that works

  1. Record the soundcheck. Even twenty minutes of the band running two songs is enough to tune with.
  2. Loop one song and mix it properly: gates, compression, FX returns, the lot, at real PA volume in the empty room.
  3. Solo things you can never solo in a show. Ring out wedges against the actual vocal, not pink noise.
  4. Keep recording during the show itself. Tonight's show is tomorrow's better virtual soundcheck, and next week's festival prep for the same band.

The traps

  • Do not touch preamps during playback. The recording is post-preamp; gain changes apply to the next real input, not what you are hearing.
  • Watch the clock rate. Console and computer must agree on 48 kHz, and the console is usually the leader.
  • Mind the PA. A muted master on the desk does not mute a solo bus routed to wedges on stage while the crew is on it.
  • Label the takes. A folder of sessions called untitled-3 defeats the entire exercise.

The rig that does all this is the same rig that records the show. Build it once, patched both ways, and every gig you record becomes rehearsal material for the next one.
